# Context and Recap
The tight binding model can be considered as a special case of [[Bloch Electrons]]:
![[Bloch Electrons#^9dd1a2]]

In the tight binding model, the periodic potential is generated by a [[Crystal Lattice]] of atoms, *where the electrons are tightly bound to an atom at each moment in time - they are in an energy orbital close enough to the atom such that they are "well localized". 

Due to our assumptions, we find that the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian have definite energy *but also definite average (crystal) momentum*, and they must be linear combinations of basic "building blocks". Each building block is the symmetric superposition of the orbitals of given type over all the crystal sites. It is clearer to think of the eigensolutions as waves, and the velocity is deduced from the dispersion relation: $$\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{ \partial \mathcal{E}_{n}(\mathbf{k}) }{ \partial \mathbf{k} }$$
We find that despite the electrons being tightly bound and having but a small chance to hop between neighbors, the metal can be infinitely conducting - the eigenstates have a definite velocity that doesn't change in time!
# Formal Explanation
Say we have a crystal lattice $\mathbf{R}$ of [[Hydrogen Atom in Quantum Mechanics|hydrogen-like atoms]]. Denoting by $V(\mathbf{r})$ the potential of a single electron in a hydrogen-like atom centered at the origin, we would get the true Hamiltonian:

$$\mathcal{H} = \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} + \sum_{\mathbf{R}}V(\mathbf{r-R})$$ ^0ba4a1

This is a periodic Hamiltonian, with eigenbasis according to [[Bloch Electrons#^62cd65]] . The eigenbasis thus can be labeled by the *crystal* momentum $\mathbf{k}$ and the band index $n$. 

Say the solutions to a hydrogen-like atom centered at the origin are $\ket{\phi_{n}}$, $n$ representing here the energy level. Then the solutions $\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R_{0}}}}$ to the same atom centered at a different location $\mathbf{R_{0}}$ have:
$$\bra{\mathbf{r}}\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R_{0}}}} = \bra{\mathbf{r-R_{0}}} \ket{\phi_{n}}   $$
*Let's assume for the moment that these single-atom solutions form an orthonormal basis $B$ of our Hilbert space.* This says two things physically: firstly (basis), the deterministic state of a single-electron in the crystal is fully described by the orbitals in which it partakes. Second (orthonormal), no two orbitals overlap - if an atom is definitely in one orbital, then it definitely isn't in another orbital.  

*However, by [[Bloch Electrons#^62cd65]], **we already see that this basis is not an eigenbasis, because it doesn't satisfy the periodicity of the crystal, namely for each $\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R_{0}}}}$ there should exist some $\mathbf{k}$ such that:***
$$\bra{\mathbf{r+R}} \ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R_{0}}}} \stackrel{?}{=} e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}\bra{\mathbf{r}}\ket{{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R_{0}}}}}   $$
***We see that the eigenstates must be states in which the orbital is not definite.***

If indeed as we assumed the single-atom solutions form a basis, then the crystal solutions can be represented as a linear combination of them. *Thus, from taking into account the symmetries of the lattice (Bloch periodicity requirement) and the assumption that $B$ is a basis, the basic building blocks for solutions of $\mathcal{H}$  must[^1] be*:
$$\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{ N }}\sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R}}}  $$
Where there are $N$ lattice sites. *This is already a strange result. If our system has one orbital, then we only have one energy eigenstate? But self-adjoint operators are necessarily diagonalizable..* If we have a lattice basis or multiple orbitals per atom, then the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian will be linear combinations of these Bloch states. Moving on to compute the energy states, we need to use our basis:
$$\begin{align}
  \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\mathcal{H}\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}}  & = \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{p^2}}{2m} + V(\mathbf{r-R_{2}}) \right]\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}} + \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{R'}\neq \mathbf{R}_{2}}V(\mathbf{r-R'})\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}}  \\
 &  \equiv \epsilon_{n_{2}} \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}} +   \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\Delta U\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}}  \\
 & = \epsilon_{n_{2}}\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}\delta_{\mathbf{R_{1},R_{2}}} + \bra{\phi_{n_{1},\mathbf{R_{1}}}}\Delta U\ket{\phi_{n_{2},\mathbf{R}_{2}}}  
\end{align}$$
Evidently, this representation should not depend on $\mathbf{R}_{i}$ individually but just on their difference $\Delta \mathbf{R}$ and we can write:
$$H_{n_{1},n_{2},\Delta \mathbf{R}} = \epsilon_{n_{1}}\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}} - t(n_{1},n_{2},\Delta \mathbf{R})$$
Where $t$ is named an overlap integral and there is no good reason for the minus sign. Then:
$$\begin{align}
E(\mathbf{k},n) & \equiv \bra{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}\mathcal{H}\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}   \\
 & = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{R,R'}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot(R-R')}}\bra{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R'}}}\mathcal{H}\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R}}} \\
 & = \epsilon_{n} - \sum_{\Delta \mathbf{R} \neq 0} e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot\Delta R}}\cdot t(n, \Delta \mathbf{R}) \\
 & \stackrel{(*)}{=} \epsilon_{n} - \sum_{\Delta \mathbf{R} \neq 0} \cos(\mathbf{k\cdot\Delta R}) \cdot t(n, \Delta \mathbf{R})\\
\end{align}$$
And (\*) because the energy must be real, and assuming $t$ is real or at least only depend on $n,\lvert \Delta \mathbf{R} \rvert$. 

Next, we *assume* a model where  $t(n,\Delta\mathbf{R})\neq {0}$ only for first-order displacements, i.e. nearest neighbors of $\mathbf{0}$.  If we apply the above to 1D lattice of spacing $a$ with a single $s$-orbital, and assume $t(\mathbf{R}) = t(R)$ it follows: $$E(\mathbf{k}) = E(k) = \epsilon_{s} +2t_{s}(a)\cos(ka)$$
# Lattice with Basis
The procedure is the same as above, the only difference being that different basis items can contribute differently to the orbital superposition. Denoting by $\ket{\phi_{n,\mathbf{R}}^{(i)}}$ the n-th orbital of the i-th basis item associated with lattice point $\mathbf{R}$, we get:
$$\ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}} = \sum_{i\text{ basis}}\sum_{n}c_{n,\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}^{(i)}} \stackrel{\text{brevity}}{\equiv}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}\ket{\psi_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}} $$
Where for brevity $\alpha$ indexes both the crystal basis and its orbitals. Then:
$$\begin{align}
\mathcal{H}\ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}}  & = E(\mathbf{k})\ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}} \\
 & \implies   \\
E(\mathbf{k})c_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}& = \sum_{\alpha'}c_{\alpha',\mathbf{k}}\cdot\bra{\psi_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}} \mathcal{H}\ket{\psi_{\alpha',\mathbf{k}}} \\
 & = c_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}\epsilon_{\alpha} + \sum_{\alpha'} c_{\alpha',\mathbf{k}}\cdot\bra{\psi_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}} \mathcal{H}\ket{\psi_{\alpha',\mathbf{k}}} 
\end{align}  $$
*Our approximating assumptions led us to the final simplification: each unique orbital type (i.e. basis item + energy level) has one definite energy state. The energy eigenstates are linear combinations of these building blocks, which can be solved for by a reduced Hamiltonian diagonalization*. All in all we'll be left with 
$$\sum_{\alpha'} \left[\left(E(\mathbf{k})-\epsilon_{\alpha}\right)\delta_{\alpha,\alpha'}+\beta_{\alpha,\alpha'}+\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}\neq0}t_{\alpha,\alpha'}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{R}}\right]c_{\alpha',\mathbf{k}}=0$$
Or written in matrix notation over the $\alpha$ representation:
$$\left[\underbrace{ \text{diag}(\vec{\epsilon}) - \beta - \sum_{\Delta \mathbf{R}\neq \mathbf{0}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot\Delta R}} t(\Delta \mathbf{R}) }_{ \mathbf{H} }\right]\vec{c_{\mathbf{k}}} = E(\mathbf{k})\vec{c_{\mathbf{k}}}$$
The entries $\beta_{\alpha,\alpha'}$ depict the transition probability $\alpha'\to\alpha$ (amplitudes) *for orbitals in the same atom*. And ${} t_{\alpha,\alpha'}(\Delta \mathbf{R}) {}$ depict the transition probability $\alpha'\to\alpha$ for atoms $\Delta \mathbf{R}$ apart, *where $\Delta \mathbf{R}$ is a crystal lattice displacement* (the extra displacement $\delta \mathbf{R}$ between different basis items is accounted for by the index $\alpha,\alpha'$).

# Slightly Different Perspective
Consider again the Hamiltonian ![[#^0ba4a1]]
Assume that the lattice distances are large enough such that two things occur at a point $\mathbf{r}$: 
1. $\sum_{R}V(\mathbf{r-R})\approx V(\mathbf{r-R_{0}})$ where $\mathbf{R_{0}}$ is the nearest lattice point to $\mathbf{r}$, i.e. the "support" of $V$ is small enough wrt the lattice distances such that at each lattice point we only feel one atomic potential. We can say that the potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ is well localized. ^8bb183
2. The wavefunction $\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})$ corresponding to $V(\mathbf{r})$ have a small enough support, such that: $\mathcal{H}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r}) \approx \epsilon_{n}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})$, or equivalently $\sum_{\mathbf{R}}V(\mathbf{r-R})\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})\approx V(\mathbf{r})\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r})$. We can say the the atomic wavefunctions are well localized, i.e. they only feel the potential of the nearest lattice point.

In such a case, we supposedly completely solved $\mathcal{H}$. But first of all, our eigensolutions are not periodic, which defies [[Bloch Electrons#^62cd65]] . Secondly, we must take into account some interaction between lattice points for the model to be worth something. 

Fixing the first problem will add the notion of crystal momentum to the model. Indeed, the only way to make the solutions periodic while keeping the eigenvalues is to construct: $$\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) \equiv \sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r-R})$$
To fix the second, we should modify the requirement in [[#^8bb183]]: 
$$\sum_{\mathbf{R}}V(\mathbf{r-R})\approx V(\mathbf{r-R_{0}}) + \Delta U(\mathbf{\mathbf{r}})$$ Where we expect the correction $\Delta U$ to be small. But this means that we must change the functions composing $\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}$! But keeping the periodicity:
$$\psi_{\mathbf{k}} = \sum_\mathbf{R}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}f(\mathbf{r-R})$$
Notice that $\psi$ no longer has a $n$ subscript, because we can't yet label its energy - we haven't determined it!

In other words, to have the right periodicity $\psi_{\mathbf{k}}$ must have the form of a sum with exponents over the lattice, and $f$ is to be determined, but generally is not expected to just be the atomic eigenfunction $\phi_{n}$. If indeed the perturbation $\Delta U$ is small, we may suspect that $\Delta U\phi_{n}$ will still be well-localized, but now may have some probability to be with a lower or higher energy, i.e. $\bra{\phi_{m}}\Delta U\ket{\phi_{n}}\neq {0}$. So we can model $\phi_{n}$ as a linear combination of the new (atomic) eigenstates, and hence the new atomic eigenstates will be linear combinations of the original ones! All in all:
$$\psi_{\mathbf{k}} = \sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}\sum_{n}b_{n}\phi_{n}(\mathbf{r-R}) \implies \ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}} = \sum_{n}b_{n}\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}  $$
*When adding the perturbation $\Delta U$, the new eigensolution ${} \ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}} {}$will be a linear combination of our former periodic solutions $\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}$. The splitting into energy bands (depicting a subscript $n_{0}$ over the range of crystal momenta k) will return when we try to solve $H\ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}}=E(\mathbf{k})\ket{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}} \implies \det(H-E_{\mathbf{k}}I)=0$ which is a polynomial with several discrete solutions.* Noticing:
$$\begin{align}
H\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}} &  = \epsilon_{n}\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}} + \Delta U\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}} \\
 \implies \bra{\psi_{m,\mathbf{k}}} H \ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}} &  = \epsilon_{n}\delta_{mn} + \sum_{\mathbf{R,R'}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot(R-R')}}\bra{m,\mathbf{R'}} \Delta U\ket{n,\mathbf{R}} \\
 & = \epsilon_{n}\delta_{nm}+\underbrace{ N }_{ \text{lattice points} }\sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k\cdot R}}\bra{m,\mathbf{0}} \Delta U\ket{n,\mathbf{R}} 
\end{align}   $$
____

[^1]: There are two statements here. First, we say that $\ket{\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}}$ is only composed of the $n-$th orbitals/$n$-th single-atom energy levels. Second, all n-th orbitals contribute the same weight. Once you assume the first, the second follows from symmetry of the lattice.
